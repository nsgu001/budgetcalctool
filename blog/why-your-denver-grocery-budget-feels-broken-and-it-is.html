Why Your Denver Grocery Budget Feels Broken (And It Is)

By Marcus Thompson • June 13, 2026 • 6 min read
Marcus Thompson • June 13, 2026 • 6 min read

Denver's inflation rate is 4.2% — a full point above the national average. Your grocery bill isn't lying to you.

I've been staring at my own receipts for the past six months, and honestly? It's depressing. A box of cereal that cost $3.99 in 2024 is now $5.29. Ground beef went from $5.99 a pound to $7.49. I'm not talking about organic, fancy stuff. Just regular grocery store staples.

My wife Rachel and I used to spend about $800 a month on groceries for our family of four. Now we're pushing $1,100. That's a $300 hit. For what? The same food.

Look, I'm a financial counselor. I've helped hundreds of Denver families with their budgets. And I'm telling you — this isn't your fault. Food inflation in Denver is running at around 5.2% year-over-year, according to the latest CPI data. That's higher than the national grocery inflation rate of 4.6%. Something's off.

Here's what I've learned from looking at my own spending and my clients':

  • Meat and dairy are up the most. I've switched to buying ground turkey instead of beef when it's on sale. Not ideal, but it saves about $10-15 a week.
  • Store brands are no longer just cheaper — they're sometimes the only affordable option. I used to be picky. Now I buy the store brand almost everything.
  • Bulk buying isn't always a deal anymore. Costco membership? Still worth it. But I've started splitting bulk packs with my neighbor to avoid waste.

I remember when I first noticed the jump. It was February. I looked at my credit card statement and saw $1,200 at King Soopers. I literally said out loud, "What the hell?" Rachel heard me from the kitchen. She just sighed. She already knew.

This is the part where I tell you there's a magic fix. There isn't. But there are small adjustments that add up.

Here's what's actually worked for my family:

1. Meal planning around sale ads. I used to plan meals first, then shop. Now I check the King Soopers and Sprouts ads on Wednesday night, then plan my week. It takes an extra 20 minutes and saves about $30-40 a week.

2. Buying frozen vegetables instead of fresh. Frozen broccoli is $1.89 a bag. Fresh is $3.49. Nutritionally, they're almost identical. Plus frozen doesn't go bad when you forget it in the fridge. (I always forget.)

3. Cutting back on name-brand snacks. My kids didn't notice when I switched from name-brand granola bars to store brand. I'm not telling them. Sorry, kids.

I'm not going to pretend this solves everything. Denver's inflation is real, and it's hitting grocery budgets harder than almost any other category. But I'd rather be honest with you than sell you a fantasy.

One more thing: don't beat yourself up if you're spending more. You're not bad at budgeting. The numbers are just against you right now. Adjust where you can, forgive yourself where you can't, and keep going.
